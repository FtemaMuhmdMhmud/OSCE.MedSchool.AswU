Eyelid

▼
1Microscopic anatomy of the eyelid VIP★

Layers (anterior → posterior)

  • Skin — thinnest in body, no subcutaneous fat
  • Subcutaneous areolar tissue — loose, allows rapid oedema
  • Muscle layer — Orbicularis oculi (closure, CN VII)
  • Submuscular areolar tissue
  • Fibrous layer — Tarsal plate + orbital septum; contains Meibomian glands
  • Smooth muscle — Müller's muscle (sympathetic, 2 mm lift)
  • Conjunctiva — posterior-most, palpebral portion

Special glands

  • Meibomian glands — sebaceous, in tarsal plate → lipid layer of tear film
  • Glands of Zeis — modified sebaceous at lash follicles
  • Glands of Moll — modified sweat glands at lid margin
2Classification of ptosis
  • Neurogenic — 3rd nerve palsy (complete + dilated pupil), Horner syndrome (partial + miosis)
  • Myogenic — Myasthenia gravis (fatigable), CPEO, myotonic dystrophy
  • Aponeurotic — Senile/involutional (most common in adults), post-surgical, post-traumatic
  • Mechanical — lid oedema, tumour, dermatochalasis
  • Congenital — levator aponeurosis dysgenesis (most common overall)
  • Pseudoptosis — phthisis bulbi, small globe, hypotropia
3Causes of ptosis ★

Based on the classification above — key causes to memorise:

  • Congenital levator dystrophy
  • Involutional/aponeurotic (most common in adults)
  • Myasthenia gravis — fatigable, variable, worse end of day, bilateral, Tensilon test +ve
  • 3rd nerve palsy — complete ptosis + dilated pupil + EOM palsy (eye down & out)
  • Horner syndrome — partial ptosis (2 mm) + miosis + anhidrosis + enophthalmos
4Causes (types) of blepharitis VIP

Anterior blepharitis

  • Staphylococcal — hard collarette scales, ulcers at lash bases, madarosis
  • Seborrhoeic — soft greasy scales, associated seborrhoeic dermatitis

Posterior blepharitis (MGD)

  • Meibomian gland dysfunction → inspissated secretions → lipid-deficient tears → dry eye
  • Rosacea-associated (most common underlying cause)

Management

Lid hygiene, warm compresses, topical antibiotics (chloramphenicol), oral doxycycline for MGD

5Fate of chalazion VIP
  • Spontaneous resolution (common in children, weeks–months)
  • Persistence as hard painless nodule (chronic lipogranuloma of Meibomian gland)
  • Secondary infection → internal hordeolum (acute, painful, pointing inward)
  • Skin breakdown → external pointing + granuloma polyposum
  • Recurrence at same or different site
  • Sebaceous cell carcinoma — if recurrent/atypical → always biopsy!

Treatment: Hot compresses first → if >4 weeks → Incision & Curettage (I&C)

6Disorders of lashes
  • Trichiasis — normal follicle position but lash grows inward → corneal abrasion (trachoma, blepharitis)
  • Distichiasis — extra row of lashes from Meibomian orifices (congenital or acquired e.g. SJS)
  • Madarosis — loss of lashes (leprosy, alopecia, SLE, severe blepharitis)
  • Poliosis — white lashes (VKH, sympathetic ophthalmia, vitiligo)
  • Trichomegaly — abnormally long lashes (HIV, cyclosporine, bimatoprost)
7DD of lagophthalmos (incomplete lid closure)
  • 7th nerve (facial) palsy — Bell's palsy, parotid tumour, mastoiditis (commonest)
  • Proptosis/exophthalmos — mechanical inability to close (thyroid eye disease)
  • Lid scarring — post-chemical burn, trachoma, pemphigoid
  • Post-blepharoplasty — over-resection of skin
  • Nocturnal lagophthalmos — incomplete closure during sleep
  • Symblepharon

Consequence: exposure keratopathy → ulceration. Rx: lubricants, lid taping, tarsorrhaphy

8DD of bilateral ptosis
  • Myasthenia gravis (most important to exclude)
  • Congenital bilateral ptosis
  • CPEO (chronic progressive external ophthalmoplegia)
  • Myotonic dystrophy
  • Blepharophimosis syndrome
  • Bilateral involutional/aponeurotic ptosis
  • Bilateral dermatochalasis (pseudoptosis)
  • Bilateral 3rd nerve palsies (rare)

Lacrimal System

▼
1Components of the lacrimal system

Secretory apparatus

  • Main lacrimal gland — in lacrimal fossa, superotemporal orbit; secretes reflex tears
  • Accessory glands of Krause & Wolfring — in fornices; basic/basal secretors
  • Goblet cells (conjunctiva) — mucin layer
  • Meibomian glands — lipid/oily layer (prevents evaporation)

Drainage (nasolacrimal apparatus)

  1. Lacrimal puncta (upper & lower) → canaliculi → common canaliculus → Lacrimal sac → Nasolacrimal duct → Inferior meatus of nose

Valve of Hasner — at NLD opening; failure to open at birth → CNLDO

2DD of epiphora (watering eye) VIP

A — Hypersecretion (increased tear production)

  • Reflex: corneal FB, trichiasis, blepharitis, conjunctivitis, uveitis, dry eye (paradoxical)
  • Emotional
  • Crocodile tears (aberrant 7th nerve regeneration)

B — Outflow obstruction (lacrimal drainage failure)

  • Punctal ectropion / stenosis / atresia
  • Canalicular obstruction (HSV, drugs: 5-FU, docetaxel; trauma)
  • Dacryocystitis / Mucocoele
  • CNLDO (congenital — valve of Hasner failure)
  • NLD block (adult — idiopathic, rhinitis, sinus disease, trauma)

Investigations: dye disappearance test, syringing & probing, dacryocystography (DCG)

Orbit / Proptosis

▼
1Causes of proptosis

Inflammatory

  • Orbital cellulitis, orbital pseudotumour, thyroid eye disease (Graves')

Vascular

  • Carotid-cavernous fistula (pulsatile), orbital varix (intermittent), capillary/cavernous haemangioma

Neoplastic

  • Primary: dermoid cyst, lacrimal gland tumour, optic glioma, meningioma
  • Secondary: metastases (breast, lung), direct extension from sinuses

Endocrine

  • Thyroid ophthalmopathy — commonest cause of bilateral proptosis in adults

Traumatic

  • Retrobulbar haemorrhage, orbital emphysema
2Classification of proptosis
  • By side: Unilateral / Bilateral
  • By onset: Acute / Chronic / Intermittent
  • By direction: Axial (retrobulbar) / Non-axial (displaced sideways)
  • By pulsation: Pulsatile (CCF, encephalocele) / Non-pulsatile
  • By reducibility: Reducible (varix, intermittent) / Irreducible
3Clinical picture of dysthyroid (Graves') ophthalmopathy
  • Lid signs: Lid retraction (Dalrymple), lid lag on downgaze (von Graefe), stare
  • Soft tissue: Periorbital oedema, chemosis, conjunctival injection
  • Proptosis: Bilateral, axial, non-pulsatile, non-reducible
  • EOM restriction: Inferior rectus most often → hypotropia, vertical diplopia
  • Cornea: Exposure keratopathy (lagophthalmos)
  • Optic nerve: Compressive optic neuropathy at orbital apex → visual loss

NO Exophthalmos (note: proptosis and exophthalmos differ; exophthalmos = bilateral, endocrine)

4Causes of enophthalmos VIP
  • Orbital blow-out fracture (floor/medial wall) — increased orbital volume
  • Phthisis bulbi (shrunken, atrophic eye)
  • Scirrhous breast cancer metastasis (fibrosis contracts orbit)
  • Dehydration / cachexia (orbital fat loss)
  • Post-orbital fat necrosis
  • Horner syndrome (apparent enophthalmos — ptosis + miosis)
5DD of acute proptosis VIP

Acute inflammatory

  • Orbital cellulitis — red, hot, painful, fever, restricted EOM, chemosis, visual threat
  • Cavernous sinus thrombosis (bilateral, septic, very ill)

Acute traumatic

  • Retrobulbar haemorrhage — ocular emergency → lateral canthotomy
  • Orbital emphysema (crackling on palpation)

Acute vascular

  • Ruptured orbital varix (worsens with Valsalva)
6DD of painful proptosis
  • Orbital cellulitis
  • Orbital pseudotumour (responds dramatically to steroids)
  • Carotid-cavernous fistula (bruit, dilated episcleral veins)
  • Retrobulbar haemorrhage
  • Infected mucocoele
  • Posterior scleritis
7DD of bilateral proptosis
  • Thyroid eye disease (Graves') — commonest
  • Lymphoma
  • Bilateral orbital metastases
  • Bilateral orbital cellulitis / cavernous sinus thrombosis
  • Craniosynostosis (shallow orbits)
  • Bilateral orbital pseudotumour
8DD of pulsatile proptosis
  • Carotid-cavernous fistula (CCF) — pulsatile, bruit audible, chemosis, dilated episcleral veins (commonest)
  • Encephalocele — bone defect posteriorly, soft, compressible, brain herniation (no bruit)
  • AV malformation of orbit
  • Aneurysm of ophthalmic artery

Conjunctiva

▼
1Classification of conjunctivitis
  • Infective: Bacterial, Viral (adenovirus, HSV, EKC), Chlamydial (trachoma, inclusion conj.)
  • Allergic: SAC, PAC, VKC, AKC, GPC
  • Autoimmune: Ocular cicatricial pemphigoid, Stevens-Johnson syndrome
  • Toxic/Chemical
  • Neonatal: Ophthalmia neonatorum
2Trachoma — CP, Complications, Treatment VIP

MacCallan Classification

  • Stage I: Early follicular hypertrophy (upper tarsal)
  • Stage IIa: Mature follicles (immature scarring)
  • Stage IIb: Follicles + papillae
  • Stage III: Follicles + scarring (Herbert's pits at limbus)
  • Stage IV: Healed (Arlt's line scar, no active inflammation)

Clinical picture

  • Mucopurulent discharge, upper lid follicles, superior pannus, Herbert's pits

Complications

  • Entropion → trichiasis → corneal ulcer → leucoma → blindness
  • Dry eye (goblet cell destruction), symblepharon, ptosis, dacryocystitis

Treatment

  • Medical: Azithromycin 1 g single oral dose OR Tetracycline eye ointment × 6 weeks
  • Surgical: Bilamellar tarsal rotation (BLTR) for trichiasis/entropion
  • WHO SAFE strategy: Surgery, Antibiotics, Facial cleanliness, Environmental improvement
3DD of serpiginous corneal ulcer
  • Streptococcus pneumoniae (80%) — typical serpiginous (creeping) hypopyon ulcer
  • Moraxella liquefaciens (elderly, alcoholics)
  • Staphylococcal keratitis
  • Other Gram-positive cocci (atypical)
4DD of pannus VIP
  • Trachoma — superior pannus (most common)
  • VKC (vernal keratoconjunctivitis)
  • Phlyctenular keratoconjunctivitis
  • Rosacea keratitis
  • Superior limbic keratoconjunctivitis (SLK)
  • Contact lens-related (contact lens-induced superior limbic keratoconjunctivitis)
5DD of follicular conjunctivitis
  • Viral conjunctivitis (adenovirus — most common)
  • Chlamydial (trachoma, inclusion conjunctivitis)
  • Molluscum contagiosum lid lesion
  • Toxic/drug-induced (pilocarpine, neomycin, idoxuridine)
  • Parinaud's oculoglandular syndrome
6DD of papillae
  • VKC — giant cobblestone papillae (upper tarsal plate)
  • GPC — giant papillary conjunctivitis (contact lens users)
  • Bacterial conjunctivitis
  • Allergic conjunctivitis (seasonal)
  • Trachoma stage II

Papillae = vascularised (central vessel); Follicles = avascular (lymphoid, no central vessel)

8DD of dry eye VIP

Aqueous-deficient dry eye

  • Sjögren syndrome (primary / secondary — RA, SLE)
  • Non-Sjögren: aging, lacrimal gland destruction (sarcoid, lymphoma, radiation)

Evaporative dry eye

  • Meibomian gland dysfunction (most common cause overall)
  • Lagophthalmos, blink abnormality, contact lenses

Mucin-deficiency

  • Chemical burn, SJS, vitamin A deficiency, trachoma
9DD of ocular discharge
  • Purulent — bacterial conjunctivitis (Staph, Strep, Gonococcus)
  • Mucopurulent — chlamydial, bacterial
  • Watery/serous — viral, allergic
  • Mucoid (stringy) — VKC, dry eye
  • Bloody — gonococcal, trauma, haemorrhagic conjunctivitis (enterovirus 70)
10Gross and minute anatomy of conjunctiva

Gross anatomy

Palpebral (lines lids) → Fornices (superior & inferior) → Bulbar (covers sclera to limbus)

Microscopic layers

  • Epithelium — stratified columnar with goblet cells (mucin secretors), more in fornix
  • Substantia propria — Adenoid layer (lymphoid follicles) + Fibrous layer (vessels, nerves)
11Membranous conjunctivitis VIP

True membrane (bleeds when removed — necrotic epithelium)

  • Diphtheria (Corynebacterium diphtheriae)
  • Stevens-Johnson syndrome
  • β-haemolytic Streptococcus

Pseudomembrane (peels off cleanly without bleeding)

  • Adenoviral (EKC)
  • Vernal keratoconjunctivitis
  • Ligneous conjunctivitis

Cornea

▼
1Layers of cornea ★
  1. Epithelium — 5 layers, stratified squamous non-keratinised; regenerates from limbal stem cells
  2. Bowman's membrane — modified stroma; cannot regenerate (leaves scar)
  3. Stroma — 90% of thickness; regular collagen type I lamellae → transparency
  4. Descemet's membrane — tough basement membrane of endothelium; can bulge out (descemetocele)
  5. Endothelium — single layer of hexagonal cells; Na/K pump keeps cornea dehydrated; cannot regenerate
2Classification of corneal conditions
  • Congenital: megalocornea, microcornea, sclerocornea
  • Inflammatory: keratitis (bacterial, viral, fungal, protozoal, interstitial)
  • Degenerative: keratoconus, band keratopathy, Arcus senilis
  • Dystrophies: anterior, stromal, posterior
  • Trauma: abrasion, laceration, chemical burn
  • Opacities: nebula, macula, leucoma, leucoma adherent
3Bacterial corneal ulcer — PF, CP, Complications, Treatment VIP

Predisposing factors

  • Low immunity: DM, old age, malnutrition
  • Loss of corneal sensation (5th nerve lesion, HSV)
  • Dry eye, lagophthalmos
  • Loss of intact corneal epithelium

Causative organisms

  • Bacterial: Pseudomonas (contact lens), Pneumococcus, Staph, Strep, Gonorrhoea
  • Viral: HSV; Fungal: Aspergillus; Protozoal: Acanthamoeba

Clinical picture

Symptoms: Pain, blepharospasm, photophobia, profuse lacrimation, poor vision

Signs: Lid oedema, conjunctival + ciliary injection, grey ulcer (loss of lustre), AC flare, hypopyon, muddy lens, miotic pupil

Complications

Non-perforating: 2° iridocyclitis, 2° glaucoma, descemetocele, corneal opacities, keratectasia

Perforating: Leucoma adherent, anterior staphyloma, infections, ant. polar cataract, hypotony

Other: Complicated cataract, endophthalmitis

Treatment

Local: Atropine 1% (cycloplegia) + Broad-spectrum AB (ciprofloxacin monotherapy OR fortified drops dual therapy) + Hot fomentation (no bandage in conjunctivitis)

General: Bed rest, avoid straining, systemic AB, Vitamins A & C

Surgical: Cauterisation, conjunctival flap, paracentesis, therapeutic keratoplasty, tarsorrhaphy

4Causes of neurotrophic keratitis
  • Herpes simplex keratitis (most common)
  • Herpes zoster ophthalmicus
  • 5th nerve lesion — tumours, trigeminal neuralgia surgery
  • Leprosy, diabetes mellitus
  • Riley-Day syndrome (familial dysautonomia)
  • Chemical burns, contact lens overuse
5Causes of interstitial keratitis
  • Syphilis — congenital (salmon patch, ghost vessels); commonest cause
  • Tuberculosis
  • Herpes simplex (disciform keratitis)
  • Herpes zoster
  • Leprosy, brucellosis, onchocerciasis
6Corneal dystrophies
  • Anterior: Meesmann (epithelial), Reis-Bückler (Bowman's)
  • Stromal: Lattice (most common stromal), Granular, Macular
  • Posterior: Fuchs endothelial dystrophy (most common overall), PPMD, CHED

All: bilateral, hereditary, progressive, avascular

7Corneal opacities
  • Nebula — faint; only seen with focal illumination at slit lamp
  • Macula — denser; visible with oblique illumination
  • Leucoma — dense white; visible in daylight, blocks vision
  • Leucoma adherent — leucoma with adherent prolapsed iris
  • Anterior staphyloma — ectatic (thinned) leucoma lined by uveal tissue; iris prolapsed
8DD of photophobia
  • Corneal conditions: abrasion, ulcer, FB, keratitis
  • Uveitis / iridocyclitis
  • Acute angle-closure glaucoma
  • Meningitis
  • Congenital glaucoma (buphthalmos) + photophobia + epiphora triad
  • Albinism (congenital)
  • Migraine aura
9Causes of corneal transparency ★

Epithelium

  • Non-keratinised
  • Covered by smooth tear film

Stroma

  • Regular arrangement of collagen bundles (lattice/destructive interference theory)
  • Non-myelinated nerves
  • Avascular, no lymphatics

Endothelium

  • Endothelial pump maintains dehydration (cornea ~78% water)
10Causes of corneal ulcer (overview)

See Q3 for PF and organisms. Summary: bacterial (Pseudomonas, Pneumococcus), viral (HSV), fungal (Aspergillus), protozoal (Acanthamoeba). Source: contact lens, conjunctivitis, blepharitis, dacryocystitis.

11Types of viral corneal ulcers

Herpes Simplex virus (HSV)

  • Dendritic ulcer — branching with terminal end bulbs (pathognomonic for HSV)
  • Disciform keratitis — central disc-shaped stromal oedema (immune mediated)

Herpes Zoster (VZV)

  • Pseudo-dendritic ulcer — without terminal buds
  • Crusting/coarse mucus plaques
  • Neurotrophic ulcer

Others

  • Limbal ulcer, trophic ulcer
12Treatment of Acanthamoeba corneal ulcer
  • Topical Brolene (propamidine isethionate) + Neomycin
  • Cycloplegics
  • Therapeutic keratoplasty in resistant cases

Prevention: avoid tap water with contact lenses, use proper lens solutions

13Complications of corneal ulcer VIP★

A — Non-perforating

  • Secondary iridocyclitis
  • Secondary glaucoma
  • Descemetocele
  • Corneal opacities (nebula → macula → leucoma)
  • Keratectasia

B — Perforating

  • Leucoma adherent (iris plugs perforation)
  • Anterior staphyloma
  • Infections (endophthalmitis)
  • Anterior polar cataract
  • Hypotony

C — Other

  • Complicated cataract, endophthalmitis
14Hypopyon corneal ulcer — CP, complications, treatment

Hypopyon = sterile pus layering in inferior AC (due to toxins from organisms)

Typical cause

Streptococcus pneumoniae → serpiginous (creeping) ulcer

Features

  • Creeping ulcer edge, grey necrotic floor, hypopyon, severe pain, ciliary injection

Treatment

Same as bacterial corneal ulcer (Q3). Hypopyon resolves with treatment of underlying ulcer.

15Dendritic corneal ulcer — CP & treatment

Clinical picture

  • FB sensation, watering, photophobia, reduced vision
  • Branching dendritic ulcer with terminal end bulbs
  • Stains with fluorescein (green), rose bengal (red margins)
  • Reduced corneal sensation

Treatment

  • Topical Acyclovir 3% ointment 5×/day × 14 days OR Ganciclovir gel
  • Cycloplegic; Avoid topical steroids (worsen HSV)
  • Surgical: debridement, therapeutic keratoplasty for non-healing/recurrent
16Keratoconus — CP & treatment

Clinical picture

  • Progressive central corneal ectasia → irregular myopic astigmatism → poor VA
  • Munson sign (V-shaped lower lid deformity on downgaze)
  • Fleischer ring (iron deposits at cone base — Kayser-Fleischer-like)
  • Vogt striae (vertical stress lines in deep stroma)
  • Acute hydrops (sudden stromal oedema from Descemet's rupture)

Treatment (stepwise)

  • Spectacles → Rigid gas-permeable (RGP) contact lenses → INTACS → Corneal collagen cross-linking (CXL) — halts progression → Keratoplasty (DALK or PK)
17Causes of serpiginous corneal ulcer VIP

See Q14 above. Pneumococcal (Streptococcus pneumoniae) is the classical causative organism in 80% of cases (typical/serpiginous pattern). Atypical: Staph, Strep, Moraxella.

Uvea / Iridocyclitis

▼
1Anatomy of the uvea VIP

Three parts

  • Iris — anterior; sphincter pupillae (parasympathetic, CN III) + dilator pupillae (sympathetic)
  • Ciliary body — pars plana (flat) + pars plicata (ciliary processes → aqueous production; ciliary muscle → accommodation)
  • Choroid — highly vascular; nourishes outer retinal layers; lies between RPE and sclera

Blood supply

Long & short posterior ciliary arteries + anterior ciliary arteries (all from ophthalmic artery)

2Acute iridocyclitis — Causes, Complications, Treatment VIP

Causes

  • Idiopathic (most common)
  • HLA-B27: ankylosing spondylitis, reactive arthritis, psoriatic arthritis, IBD
  • Infectious: HSV, CMV, Toxoplasma, syphilis, TB, leprosy, Lyme
  • Trauma, post-surgical
  • Sarcoidosis

Clinical picture

Symptoms: Pain, photophobia, lacrimation, blurred vision

Signs: Ciliary injection, KPs (keratic precipitates on endothelium), AC flare & cells, miosis, posterior synechiae, low IOP

Complications

  • Posterior synechiae → seclusio / occlusio pupillae → iris bombé → secondary glaucoma
  • Complicated cataract (posterior subcapsular)
  • Band keratopathy (calcium in Bowman's)
  • Phthisis bulbi (end-stage)

Treatment

  • Cycloplegics (atropine 1%) — break/prevent synechiae, relieve ciliary spasm
  • Topical steroids (prednisolone acetate) — reduce inflammation
  • Treat underlying cause
  • Systemic immunosuppressants in refractory/systemic disease
3Types of iridectomy
  • Peripheral iridectomy — breaks pupil block; used in AACG, iris bombé
  • Sector iridectomy — larger sector; old cataract technique, iris tumour
  • Optical iridectomy — central; improves vision by bypassing corneal opacity
  • Total iridectomy — entire iris; melanoma
  • Therapeutic iridectomy — prevents/treats iris bombé (occlusio pupillae)
  • Laser peripheral iridotomy (LPI) — non-surgical equivalent; preferred method
4Causes of haemorrhagic iridocyclitis / DD of hyphema VIP

Haemorrhagic iridocyclitis causes

  • Rubeosis iridis (NVI from DM, CRVO, CRAO)
  • Fuchs heterochromic iridocyclitis
  • Trauma

DD of hyphema

  • Blunt trauma (most common)
  • Post-surgical (cataract, glaucoma)
  • Rubeosis iridis
  • Fuchs heterochromic iridocyclitis
  • Haemophilia / bleeding disorders / anticoagulants
  • Juvenile xanthogranuloma (children)
5DD of atrophia bulbi
  • End-stage untreated glaucoma
  • Severe penetrating/perforating trauma
  • Chronic severe uveitis
  • Endophthalmitis
  • Long-standing retinal detachment
  • Radiation injury

Phthisis bulbi = shrunken, calcified, non-functional, soft eye with low IOP

6Uveitis classification
  • Anatomical (SUN Working Group): Anterior (iritis, iridocyclitis) / Intermediate (pars planitis) / Posterior (choroiditis, retinitis, vasculitis) / Panuveitis
  • Onset: Acute / Chronic / Recurrent
  • Morphology: Granulomatous (mutton-fat KPs, Busacca & Koeppe nodules) / Non-granulomatous (fine KPs)
  • Aetiology: Infectious / Non-infectious / Masquerade syndrome
7Treatment of hyphema
  • Bed rest + head elevation 30–45°
  • Fox shield (no pressure patching)
  • Topical steroid + cycloplegic
  • Anti-fibrinolytic — tranexamic acid or aminocaproic acid (prevents rebleed)
  • Monitor IOP; if elevated → anti-glaucoma drops (avoid pilocarpine)
  • Surgical washout if: total hyphema, raised IOP uncontrolled, corneal blood staining
  • Avoid aspirin/NSAIDs
8Treatment of hyphema (from trauma chapter overlap)

See above (Q7). Identical management principles apply for traumatic hyphema.

Crystalline Lens

▼
1Causes (types) of cataract
  • Age-related (senile) — most common; nuclear, cortical, posterior subcapsular
  • Congenital — TORCH infections, rubella (pearly nuclear), galactosaemia, Lowe syndrome
  • Traumatic — blunt (Vossius ring, rosette cataract) or perforating
  • Complicated — intraocular disease: uveitis, glaucoma, RP, high myopia (PSC)
  • Metabolic — DM (snowflake), hypocalcaemia, Wilson's disease (sunflower)
  • Drug-induced — corticosteroids (PSC), chlorpromazine (anterior capsule), amiodarone
  • Radiation — X-ray, IR, UV (glass-blower)
  • Electric shock
2Layers of lens VIP
  1. Lens capsule — elastic basement membrane; thickest anteriorly at equator
  2. Anterior subcapsular epithelium — metabolically active; source of new fibres
  3. Cortex — recently formed fibres; more hydrated
  4. Nucleus — oldest fibres; harder and yellower with age

No posterior subcapsular epithelium. Avascular — nourished by aqueous humor.

3DD of leucocoria (white pupil reflex)
  • Cataract (congenital or acquired)
  • Retinoblastoma — most important to exclude in children!
  • PHPV (persistent hyperplastic primary vitreous)
  • ROP (retinopathy of prematurity)
  • Coats disease
  • Toxocara endophthalmitis
  • Retinal detachment
  • Endophthalmitis
4Types of lens extraction
  • ICCE — Intracapsular; entire lens + capsule removed; historical; AC IOL or aphakic glasses
  • ECCE — Extracapsular; nucleus expressed, cortex aspirated, posterior capsule left; PC IOL
  • Phacoemulsification — ultrasound fragmentation + aspiration; 2.75 mm incision; gold standard
  • Manual SICS — small incision; no phaco machine; used in resource-limited settings
  • FLACS — femtosecond laser-assisted; precise capsulotomy
5Causes of complicated cataract VIP
  • Chronic uveitis (anterior or posterior) — most common cause
  • Retinitis pigmentosa (PSC)
  • High myopia (nuclear)
  • Glaucoma
  • Intraocular tumours
  • Long-standing retinal detachment
  • Essential iris atrophy

Typical appearance: posterior subcapsular cataract (PSC)

6Causes of unilateral cataract
  • Trauma — most common cause of unilateral cataract
  • Unilateral uveitis
  • Unilateral high myopia
  • PHPV
  • Unilateral intraocular tumour
  • Silicone oil (post retinal detachment surgery)
7DD of night blindness (nyctalopia)
  • Retinitis pigmentosa (most common)
  • Vitamin A deficiency
  • Congenital stationary night blindness (CSNB)
  • Choroideraemia
  • Gyrate atrophy
  • Advanced glaucoma
  • Pathological myopia
8DD of day blindness (hemeralopia)
  • Cone dystrophy / achromatopsia (rods intact, no cones)
  • Central corneal opacity
  • Dense nuclear cataract (better in dim light as pupil dilates around opacity)
  • Macular disease (cone-rich area)
  • Albinism
9Abnormalities of anterior chamber
  • Hyphaema — blood in AC (layer inferiorly)
  • Hypopyon — pus/WBCs in AC
  • AC flare & cells — protein + WBCs in uveitis
  • Shallow AC — AACG, post-op wound leak, iris bombé
  • Deep AC — aphakia, high myopia
  • Angle recession — traumatic, risk of late glaucoma
10Glaucomas in lens disease (lenticular causes of 2° glaucoma) VIP
  • Phacomorphic — intumescent (swollen) lens → pupil block → AACG
  • Phacolytic — hypermature lens leaks proteins → macrophage TM blockage → OAG type
  • Phacoanaphylactic — immune response to lens proteins post-trauma/surgery
  • Lens particle glaucoma — retained cortex post-surgery blocks TM
  • Subluxation/dislocation — pupil block or direct TM obstruction
11Effect of blunt/perforating trauma on lens VIP

Blunt trauma

  • Vossius ring (iris pigment stamped on anterior lens capsule)
  • Rosette/stellate cataract (posterior subcapsular)
  • Subluxation or dislocation

Perforating trauma

  • Traumatic cataract (rapid, after capsule rupture → aqueous entry)
  • Dislocation into AC or vitreous
  • Risk of sympathetic ophthalmia
12Lens subluxation and dislocation

Causes

  • Marfan syndrome — upward & temporal (most classic)
  • Homocystinuria — downward & nasal
  • Weill-Marchesani, hyperlysinaemia
  • Blunt trauma

Complications

  • Glaucoma, cataract, uveitis, diplopia (monocular), retinal detachment
⚡

Glaucoma

▼
1Angle structures VIP

From anterior → posterior (as seen on gonioscopy):

  1. Schwalbe's line — peripheral end of Descemet's membrane
  2. Trabecular meshwork (TM) — uveal, corneoscleral, and juxtacanalicular layers
  3. Schlemm's canal — collects aqueous → collector channels → episcleral veins
  4. Scleral spur — attachment of longitudinal ciliary muscle fibres
  5. Ciliary body band
  6. Iris root
2Acute congestive glaucoma (AACG) VIP★

Predisposing factors

  • Hypermetropia (small eye, shallow AC, narrow angle), female, elderly, Asian, family history

Precipitating factors

  • Dim light (mydriasis), emotional stress, mydriatic drops, anticholinergics, general anaesthesia

Clinical picture

Symptoms: Severe unilateral pain, headache, nausea/vomiting, coloured halos (corneal oedema), rapidly falling vision

Signs: Ciliary + conjunctival injection, steamy oedematous cornea, mid-dilated fixed oval pupil (~6 mm), very high IOP (>40 mmHg), shallow AC, closed angle on gonioscopy

Treatment (emergency)

  • IV acetazolamide (Diamox) 500 mg stat
  • IV mannitol 20% (1 g/kg)
  • Topical timolol 0.5% + pilocarpine 2–4%
  • Analgesics, antiemetics
  • Definitive: Laser peripheral iridotomy (LPI) or surgical PI when cornea clears
  • Fellow eye: prophylactic LPI
3Treatment of open-angle glaucoma (OAG)

Medical (stepwise)

  • Prostaglandin analogue (latanoprost 0.005%) — 1st line; increases uveoscleral outflow
  • Beta-blockers (timolol) — decrease aqueous production
  • Alpha-2 agonists (brimonidine)
  • Carbonic anhydrase inhibitors (dorzolamide, brinzolamide; systemic: acetazolamide)

Laser

  • SLT (selective laser trabeculoplasty) — increases TM outflow

Surgery

  • Trabeculectomy (guarded filtration procedure) ± Mitomycin C
  • Tube/shunt (Ahmed/Baerveldt valve) — refractory glaucoma
  • MIGS (minimally invasive glaucoma surgery)
4Causes of secondary ACG (SACG) & secondary OAG (SOAG)

Secondary ACG

  • Iris bombé (posterior synechiae → pupil block) → peripheral anterior synechiae
  • Rubeosis iridis / Neovascular glaucoma
  • Phacomorphic glaucoma (intumescent lens)
  • Aqueous misdirection (malignant glaucoma)

Secondary OAG

  • Pseudoexfoliation syndrome (most common secondary OAG)
  • Pigment dispersion syndrome
  • Phacolytic
  • Steroid-induced
  • Uveitic glaucoma
  • Angle recession (post-traumatic)
5Causes of resistant (refractory) glaucoma
  • Neovascular glaucoma (rubeosis iridis — DM, CRVO, CRAO)
  • Post-failed trabeculectomy (scarred bleb)
  • Uveitic glaucoma
  • Post-traumatic (angle recession)
  • Congenital/childhood glaucoma after failed goniotomy
  • Silicone oil-induced
  • ICE syndrome

Management: tube/shunt (Ahmed valve), cyclodestructive procedures (cyclodiode laser)

6Causes of rapid diminution of vision
  • CRAO (central retinal artery occlusion) — painless, sudden, profound
  • CRVO (ischaemic type)
  • Vitreous haemorrhage
  • Retinal detachment (macula-off)
  • Acute angle-closure glaucoma
  • Optic neuritis
  • Anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy (AION)
  • Wet AMD (sudden subfoveal haemorrhage)
  • Retrobulbar haemorrhage
7Visual field changes in glaucoma
  1. Enlarged blind spot
  2. Seidel scotoma (teardrop extension of blind spot)
  3. Bjerrum (arcuate) scotoma — arcs around fixation from blind spot
  4. Roenne's nasal step (at horizontal meridian)
  5. Ring/double arcuate scotoma
  6. Central & temporal islands — tunnel vision
  7. Complete field loss
8Clinical picture of buphthalmos (congenital glaucoma)
  • Large cornea (>13 mm in newborn), large eye (buphthalmos = ox eye)
  • Haab's striae — horizontal breaks in Descemet's membrane
  • Corneal oedema → photophobia, epiphora, blepharospasm (triad)
  • High IOP, optic disc cupping
  • Axial myopia (increased axial length)

Treatment: goniotomy (first-line) or trabeculotomy, then trabeculectomy

9Lenticular causes of secondary glaucoma VIP

Phacomorphic, phacolytic, phacoanaphylactic, lens particle, subluxation/dislocation — see Lens chapter Q10 for full details.

Retina

▼
1Layers of retina VIP

10 layers (from outer/choroidal side → inner/vitreous side):

  1. Retinal pigment epithelium (RPE)
  2. Photoreceptor layer (outer & inner segments of rods & cones)
  3. External limiting membrane (zonulae adherentes)
  4. Outer nuclear layer (nuclei of photoreceptors)
  5. Outer plexiform layer (photoreceptor synapses with bipolar cells)
  6. Inner nuclear layer (bipolar, Müller, amacrine, horizontal cells)
  7. Inner plexiform layer (bipolar cells synapse with ganglion cells)
  8. Ganglion cell layer
  9. Nerve fibre layer (axons of ganglion cells → optic nerve)
  10. Internal limiting membrane

Outer 4 layers nourished by choriocapillaris; inner 6 by central retinal artery

2Structures seen during fundus examination
  • Optic disc: colour, margins, cup-to-disc ratio (normal ≤0.5), vessels
  • Blood vessels: A:V ratio (normal 2:3), crossing changes, calibre irregularity
  • Macula & fovea: foveal reflex, pigmentation, exudates, haemorrhages
  • Peripheral retina: pigmentation, tears, detachment, lattice degeneration
  • Vitreous: floaters, haemorrhage, opacities
3Diabetic retinopathy — RF, stages, treatment

Risk factors

  • Duration of DM (most important), poor HbA1c control, hypertension, nephropathy, pregnancy, dyslipidaemia, anaemia

ETDRS Classification

  • NPDR: Mild (microaneurysms only) → Moderate → Severe (4-2-1 rule: haemorrhages in 4 quadrants OR venous beading in 2 quadrants OR IRMA in 1 quadrant)
  • PDR: NVD/NVE, vitreous haemorrhage, tractional retinal detachment
  • DMO/CSME: Clinically significant macular oedema at any stage

Treatment

  • Control DM, BP, lipids
  • PRP (pan-retinal photocoagulation) — PDR, severe NPDR
  • Anti-VEGF injections (bevacizumab, ranibizumab, aflibercept) — DMO
  • Focal/grid laser — CSME
  • PPV (pars plana vitrectomy) — non-clearing VH, TRD
4Classification of retinal conditions
  • Vascular: CRAO, CRVO, branch occlusions, DR, hypertensive retinopathy
  • Degenerative: AMD (dry & wet), RP, lattice degeneration, pathological myopia
  • Inflammatory: Toxoplasma, CMV retinitis, sarcoid, Birdshot
  • Detachment: Rhegmatogenous, tractional, exudative
  • Tumours: Retinoblastoma, choroidal melanoma, haemangioma, metastases
  • Hereditary dystrophies: RP, cone dystrophy, macular dystrophies (Stargardt, Best)
5Hypertensive retinopathy

Keith-Wagener-Barker classification

  • Grade I: Mild arterial narrowing, increased A:V ratio, copper wire
  • Grade II: Arteriovenous nipping (Salus, Gunn signs), silver wire
  • Grade III: Grade II + flame haemorrhages, cotton-wool spots, hard exudates (macular star)
  • Grade IV: Grade III + disc oedema (papilloedema) — malignant hypertension, life-threatening
6DD of RAPD (relative afferent pupillary defect)
  • Optic neuritis (MS, NMO)
  • CRAO
  • Ischaemic optic neuropathy (AION)
  • Optic nerve trauma/compression
  • Large/total retinal detachment
  • Severe ischaemic CRVO
  • Advanced glaucoma (unilateral)
  • Optic nerve tumour/meningioma

Detected by: swinging flashlight test → paradoxical dilation on affected side (Marcus Gunn pupil)

7DD of eye floaters
  • Posterior vitreous detachment (PVD) — Weiss ring (commonest benign cause in adults)
  • Vitreous haemorrhage (reddish/dark floaters)
  • Uveitis (inflammatory cells, pars planitis "snowballs")
  • Asteroid hyalosis (calcium-lipid deposits, unilateral, no symptoms usually)
  • Synchisis scintillans (cholesterol crystals)
  • Retinal tear/detachment — new floaters + flashes = emergency
8Diabetes and the eye VIP
  • Retina: Diabetic retinopathy (NPDR, PDR, DMO)
  • Lens: Early cataract (nuclear + PSC), snowflake cataract (young DM)
  • Iris: Rubeosis iridis → neovascular glaucoma
  • Cornea: Recurrent erosions, reduced sensation, delayed epithelial healing
  • Cranial nerves: 3rd, 6th nerve palsies (painful; 3rd in DM is pupil-sparing)
  • Refractive: Myopia (high sugar → swollen lens)
  • Glaucoma: Increased risk of POAG
9DD of cherry red spot
  • CRAO — most common; white retinal oedema + cherry red at fovea (RPE/choroid showing through)
  • Tay-Sachs disease (GM2 gangliosidosis)
  • Niemann-Pick disease
  • Sandhoff disease
  • Metachromatic leukodystrophy
  • Commotio retinae (traumatic, transient)
10Causes of sudden loss of vision
  • CRAO — painless, sudden, profound (hand motion or less)
  • CRVO (ischaemic type)
  • Vitreous haemorrhage
  • Retinal detachment (macula-off)
  • Acute angle-closure glaucoma (painful)
  • Optic neuritis (painful on movement)
  • AION — painless, on waking
  • Wet AMD sudden haemorrhage
  • Retrobulbar haemorrhage
11Causes of gradual loss of vision
  • Cataract (most common cause of blindness worldwide)
  • Glaucoma (open-angle — silent)
  • Dry AMD
  • Diabetic retinopathy (NPDR)
  • Refractive error (uncorrected)
  • Retinitis pigmentosa
  • Optic atrophy
  • Corneal opacity (trachoma)
12Fundus picture of CRAO, CRVO, RP VIP

CRAO

  • Diffuse white (milky) retinal oedema + cherry red spot at fovea, boxcarring of arteries, pale disc eventually

CRVO

  • Disc oedema, haemorrhages in all 4 quadrants ("blood and thunder" fundus), dilated tortuous veins, cotton-wool spots, macular oedema

Retinitis Pigmentosa (RP)

  • Bone-spicule pigmentation in mid-periphery, attenuated arterioles, waxy pale disc; associated posterior subcapsular cataract

Strabismus

▼
1Classification of strabismus
  • Latent (phoria): esophoria, exophoria, hyperphoria — controlled by fusion
  • Manifest (tropia): esotropia, exotropia, hypertropia — fusion broken
  • By onset: Congenital (infantile) / Acquired
  • By comitance: Comitant (equal angle all gazes) / Incomitant (paralytic — varies)
  • By direction: Horizontal / Vertical / Torsional

3Criteria of infantile esotropia
  • Onset before 6 months of age
  • Large & stable angle (>30 PD)
  • Equal/alternating fixation (no or minimal amblyopia)
  • Cross-fixation (uses each eye for opposite visual field)
  • Associated DVD (dissociated vertical deviation) in ~80%
  • Inferior oblique overaction
  • Latent nystagmus

Treatment: correct any significant refractive error → surgery (bilateral medial rectus recession)

4DD of amblyopia VIP
  • Strabismic — cortical suppression of deviating eye (most common)
  • Anisometropic — unequal refractive error between the two eyes
  • Ametropic — bilateral high refractive error (esp. hypermetropia)
  • Deprivation (stimulus) — congenital cataract, ptosis, corneal opacity (worst type)
  • Meridional — uncorrected astigmatism

Critical period: birth–7–8 years. Treatment: correct cause + occlusion of fellow eye

5DD of diplopia

Monocular diplopia (persists with one eye covered)

  • Uncorrected astigmatism, corneal irregularity, early cataract, lens subluxation

Binocular diplopia (disappears on covering one eye)

  • CN III, IV, or VI palsy
  • Thyroid eye disease (EOM restriction)
  • Myasthenia gravis (variable, fatigable)
  • Orbital blow-out fracture (inferior rectus entrapment)
  • Decompensated phoria
  • Internuclear ophthalmoplegia (MLF lesion)
6Manifestations of 3rd, 4th, 6th nerve palsy

3rd nerve (Oculomotor)

  • Complete ptosis, exotropia + hypotropia (eye down & out), dilated fixed pupil
  • DM → painful, pupil-sparing (ischaemia spares pupillary fibres on outside)
  • Surgical → pupil-involving (compression: aneurysm/uncal herniation)

4th nerve (Trochlear)

  • Vertical diplopia worse on downgaze (reading)
  • Hypertropia of affected eye
  • Compensatory head tilt away from affected side
  • Positive Bielschowsky head tilt test

6th nerve (Abducens)

  • Esotropia (medial deviation), inability to abduct
  • Horizontal diplopia, worse looking toward affected side
  • Compensatory head turn toward affected side
7Diagnosis of heterophoria VIP
  • Cover-uncover test — movement of eye on uncovering = phoria
  • Alternate cover test + prisms — quantifies the angle
  • Maddox rod test — dissociates eyes; patient sees line displaced from light dot
  • Maddox wing — measures near phoria
8Benefits of cover test
  • Detects manifest strabismus (tropia) — cover-uncover test
  • Detects latent strabismus (phoria) — alternate cover test
  • Identifies fixation preference and amblyopia
  • Quantifies deviation (with prism bar)
  • Assesses comitance (repeat in different gazes)
  • Differentiates true strabismus from pseudostrabismus

Neuro-Ophthalmology

▼
1Abnormal pupillary reactions
  • RAPD (Marcus Gunn pupil) — swinging flashlight → paradoxical dilation on affected side; afferent defect (optic nerve/retina)
  • Argyll Robertson pupil — small, irregular; accommodates but no direct/consensual light reflex; bilateral; syphilis (midbrain lesion)
  • Adie's tonic pupil — large; slow/absent light reflex; slow near response with redilation; denervation supersensitivity (dilute pilocarpine 0.1% constricts)
  • Horner syndrome — miosis + partial ptosis + anhidrosis + enophthalmos (apparent)
  • Hutchinson pupil — fixed dilated pupil from uncal herniation compressing CN III
2Causes of miosis & mydriasis

Miosis (small pupil)

  • Horner syndrome (unilateral)
  • Pontine haemorrhage (bilateral, pinpoint)
  • Argyll Robertson pupil (bilateral small)
  • Drugs: pilocarpine, opiates, organophosphates
  • Iridocyclitis (inflammatory miosis)

Mydriasis (large pupil)

  • 3rd nerve palsy (dilated, fixed — surgical)
  • Adie's tonic pupil
  • Anticholinergics (atropine, tropicamide)
  • Sympathomimetics (cocaine, adrenaline)
  • AACG (mid-dilated, fixed, oval)
  • Traumatic mydriasis (iris sphincter rupture)
3DD of RAPD

Causes of RAPD = causes of unilateral or significantly asymmetric afferent pathway lesion:

  • Optic neuritis (MS — most common in young adults)
  • CRAO
  • Ischaemic optic neuropathy (AION)
  • Optic nerve trauma/compression
  • Large/total retinal detachment
  • Severe ischaemic CRVO
  • Advanced unilateral glaucoma
  • Optic nerve tumour

Optic Nerve

▼
1Classification of optic disc/nerve conditions
  • Swollen disc: papilloedema, papillitis, AION, neuroretinitis
  • Optic atrophy: primary, secondary, consecutive, glaucomatous
  • Optic neuritis: retrobulbar, papillitis
  • Optic neuropathy: ischaemic, compressive, toxic, hereditary (Leber's)
  • Disc anomalies: coloboma, morning glory, drusen, hypoplasia
2DD of optic disc swelling VIP

Bilateral (raised ICP = papilloedema)

  • Space-occupying lesion (tumour, abscess)
  • Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (pseudotumour cerebri)
  • Meningitis, encephalitis
  • Venous sinus thrombosis
  • Hydrocephalus

Unilateral causes

  • Papillitis (optic neuritis) — MS, viral
  • AION (arteritic or non-arteritic)
  • CRVO
  • Neuroretinitis
  • Orbital tumour compressing nerve
  • Diabetic papillopathy

Pseudo-papilloedema

  • Optic disc drusen, hypermetropia, myelinated nerve fibres
3Causes of optic atrophy / DD of RAPD VIP

Primary optic atrophy (no preceding swelling)

  • Toxic: methanol (most toxic!), tobacco-alcohol, ethambutol, quinine, chloramphenicol
  • Hereditary: Leber's hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON — young males, mitochondrial), dominant optic atrophy
  • Syphilis, demyelination (MS)

Secondary optic atrophy (follows disc swelling)

  • Post-papilloedema, post-papillitis, post-AION

Consecutive optic atrophy (from retinal disease)

  • RP, CRAO, extensive chorioretinitis

Glaucomatous optic atrophy

  • Cupped disc + increased CDR + NFL defects

Compressive

  • Pituitary adenoma (bitemporal hemianopia), meningioma, optic canal fracture, thyroid eye disease
4DD of achromatopsia (colour blindness)

Congenital

  • Red-green (most common; X-linked; Daltonism) — protanopia, deuteranopia
  • Blue-yellow (tritan — autosomal dominant)
  • Complete achromatopsia (rod monochromatism) — all colours absent, nystagmus, photophobia

Acquired

  • Macular disease → blue-yellow defect
  • Optic nerve disease → red-green defect
  • Advanced glaucoma
  • Drugs: sildenafil (blue-yellow tinge), digoxin (yellow-green xanthopsia)

Eye Injuries / Trauma

▼
1Effects of blunt trauma (tennis ball injury) ★

Adnexal

  • Periorbital ecchymosis (panda/raccoon eyes)
  • Oedema, subcutaneous emphysema
  • Infraorbital nerve anaesthesia (cheek numbness)
  • Ophthalmoplegia (EOM restriction)
  • Enophthalmos (if severe blow-out fracture)
  • Blow-out fracture of orbital floor/medial wall
  • Inferior rectus entrapment → vertical diplopia

Anterior segment

  • Corneal: abrasion, stromal oedema, traumatic hyphema
  • Pupil: Vossius ring (iris pigment on lens), iridodialysis, traumatic mydriasis
  • Lens: rosette/stellate cataract (PSC), subluxation, dislocation

Posterior segment

  • Commotio retinae (Berlin's oedema) — white retinal oedema peripherally
  • Vitreous haemorrhage
  • Retinal breaks and detachment (peripheral)
  • Rupture globe (if severe)
2Management of chemical injuries VIP★

Symptoms

  • Severe pain, blurring of vision, loss of vision

Signs

  • Corneal opacity/haziness, limbal ischaemia/blanching (worst prognostic sign), conjunctival chemosis

Complications

  • Symblepharon, eyelid deformity, involutional trichiasis
  • Severe corneal opacity, corneal ulceration
  • Secondary glaucoma, phthisis

Treatment — Medical

  1. Copious irrigation with water/saline (15–30 min) — MOST URGENT STEP
  2. Topical steroids (7–10 days)
  3. Topical + systemic ascorbic acid (enhances collagen production)
  4. Topical citric acid (inhibits neutrophil chemotaxis)
  5. Topical + systemic tetracycline (inhibits collagenase & neutrophil activity)
  6. Cycloplegia (cyclopentolate — improves comfort)

Treatment — Surgical

  • Re-establish fornices (symblepharon ring)
  • Division of conjunctival bands
  • Correction of eyelid deformity / trichiasis
  • Keratoplasty / Keratoprosthesis

Alkali burns worse than acid: saponification allows deeper penetration

3Classification of eye injuries
  • Mechanical: Blunt (closed globe) / Open globe: penetrating (single wound), perforating (entry + exit), IOFB
  • Chemical: Alkali (NaOH, lime) / Acid (sulphuric)
  • Radiation: UV (welder's flash, snow blindness), IR, X-ray, laser
  • Thermal
  • Electrical
4Treatment of ruptured/perforated globe
  • Do NOT remove IOFB or apply pressure
  • Rigid Fox shield (no pad)
  • IV antibiotics (cefazolin + gentamicin) + analgesics + antiemetics
  • Tetanus prophylaxis (if not up to date)
  • Emergency surgical exploration and primary repair
  • If irreparable and no vision potential: enucleation → prevents sympathetic ophthalmia
5Treatment of hyphema

Bed rest + head elevation 30–45° + Fox shield. Topical steroid + cycloplegic. Anti-fibrinolytic (tranexamic acid). Monitor IOP. Surgical washout if: total hyphema, uncontrolled IOP, corneal blood staining.

6Ocular emergencies VIP
  • Chemical injury — copious irrigation IMMEDIATELY (most urgent)
  • Retrobulbar haemorrhage — lateral canthotomy + inferior cantholysis
  • CRAO — within 90 minutes: digital massage, IOP lowering, carbogen inhalation
  • Acute angle-closure glaucoma — IV Diamox, mannitol, pilocarpine, LPI
  • Endophthalmitis — intravitreal antibiotics (vancomycin + ceftazidime) ASAP
  • Orbital cellulitis — IV antibiotics, surgical drainage of abscess
  • Ruptured globe — Fox shield + emergency surgical repair
  • Central retinal artery occlusion
  • Carotid-cavernous fistula — urgent neurovascular intervention
