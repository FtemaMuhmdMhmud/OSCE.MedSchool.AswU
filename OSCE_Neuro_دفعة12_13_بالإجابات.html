1Causes of hypertonia & hypotonia — Compare spasticity vs rigidity — Compare sensory vs motor ataxia — Muscle doing abduction of shoulder
Causes of Hypertonia:
• Spasticity (UMNL): Stroke, MS, CP, Spinal cord injury
• Rigidity (Extrapyramidal): Parkinson's, Parkinsonism, Wilson's disease

Causes of Hypotonia: LMNL (PN, AHC disease, myopathy), Cerebellar disease, Shock (early UMNL)

Spasticity vs Rigidity:
• Spasticity: UMNL → velocity-dependent ↑ tone, "clasp-knife" phenomenon, affects flexors in UL & extensors in LL, hyperreflexia, clonus
• Rigidity: Extrapyramidal → not velocity-dependent, "lead pipe" or "cog-wheel" (if tremor superimposed), all directions, normal reflexes

Sensory vs Motor Ataxia:
• Sensory ataxia: Loss of proprioception → wide-based gait, Romberg +ve, worse in dark/eyes closed, causes: PN, posterior column lesion (tabes dorsalis, B12 def)
• Motor (Cerebellar) ataxia: Cerebellar lesion → wide-based REELING gait, Romberg -ve, no improvement with eye closure, dysmetria, dysdiadochokinesia, intention tremor

Abduction of shoulder: Deltoid (C5) – primary abductor first 15°: supraspinatus
2Examine deep reflex — Types of hypertonia — Segment of ankle & knee
Deep reflexes:
• Biceps C5,6 | Brachioradialis C5,6 | Triceps C7,8 | Knee L3,4 | Ankle S1,2

Technique: Relax patient → use tendon hammer → compare sides → grade 0-4 (0=absent, 2=normal, 4=clonus)

Types of Hypertonia:
1. Spasticity (UMNL) – clasp-knife, velocity-dependent
2. Rigidity (Extrapyramidal) – lead-pipe / cog-wheel
3. Paratonia (Gegenhalten) – frontal lobe lesion
4. Dystonia – sustained muscle contractions

Ankle reflex: S1, S2 | Knee reflex: L3, L4
3Examine CN 9,10,11,12 — Difference between absence seizures & psychomotor fits (complex partial)
CN 9 (Glossopharyngeal): Gag reflex (afferent), taste posterior 1/3 tongue, sensation pharynx
CN 10 (Vagus): Gag reflex (efferent), uvula midline, palate elevation, voice quality (nasal?), swallowing
CN 11 (Accessory): Sternomastoid power (turn head against resistance), Trapezius (shrug shoulders)
CN 12 (Hypoglossal): Tongue protrusion (deviates to lesion side in LMNL), wasting, fasciculations

4Brown-Séquard syndrome — What is it? What happens in it?
Brown-Séquard Syndrome = Hemisection of spinal cord

On side of lesion (ipsilateral):
• UMNL below lesion (spastic paresis, hyperreflexia, Babinski)
• Loss of proprioception, vibration, fine touch (posterior column)

On opposite side (contralateral):
• Loss of pain & temperature (spinothalamic – crosses at cord level)
• Normal motor & proprioception

Causes: Trauma (most common), MS, tumor, cord infarction, transverse myelitis
5Internuclear ophthalmoplegia (INO) — What is it and what do we find?
INO = Lesion of Medial Longitudinal Fasciculus (MLF)

Clinical features:
• Ipsilateral eye: fails to adduct on lateral gaze
• Contralateral eye: abducts with nystagmus
• Convergence: usually preserved (unless bilateral)

Cause in young adults: Multiple Sclerosis (MS)
Cause in elderly: Brainstem vascular disease

Pathology: MLF connects CN6 nucleus to contralateral CN3 nucleus, coordinating conjugate horizontal gaze
6Examine reflexes of upper and lower limbs — Normal findings vs with Babinski
Upper limb reflexes:
• Biceps (C5,6): flex elbow slightly, finger on biceps tendon, strike finger
• Triceps (C7,8): flex elbow 90°, strike triceps tendon above olecranon
• Brachioradialis/Supinator (C5,6): strike styloid process of radius

Lower limb reflexes:
• Knee (L3,4): patient sitting, strike patellar tendon
• Ankle (S1,2): dorsiflex foot, strike Achilles tendon

Babinski sign: Stroke lateral sole of foot → extensor plantar (dorsiflexion of big toe + fanning) = UMNL

1Muscle status + deep reflex in lower limbs with centers — Muscle wasting causes — UMNL with wasting?
Muscle tone grading: Normal / Hypotonia / Hypertonia (spastic/rigid)

Deep reflexes: Knee L3,4 | Ankle S1,2 (ankle most commonly lost in PN)

Causes of muscle wasting:
• LMNL: LMN lesion, peripheral neuropathy, myopathy, disuse atrophy
• UMNL: Wasting can occur LATE (disuse atrophy after months/years) – NOT early feature

Causes of lost ankle reflex:
• Peripheral neuropathy (most common)
• S1 radiculopathy
• Elderly (normal age-related loss)
• Diabetes, hypothyroidism
2Examine facial nerve — UMNL vs LMNL facial appearance — Parasympathetic fibers of facial nerve
Examination of facial nerve (motor):
Upper: raise eyebrows, close eyes tight, wrinkle forehead
Lower: show teeth, puff cheeks, close mouth against resistance

UMNL (Central) facial palsy:
• ONLY lower face affected (forehead spared)
• Reason: upper face has BILATERAL cortical representation
• Contralateral to lesion

LMNL (Peripheral/Bell's palsy):
• WHOLE hemifacial palsy (upper AND lower)
• Ipsilateral
• May have: hyperacusis (stapedius), loss of taste (anterior 2/3 tongue), dry eye

Parasympathetic fibers of facial nerve:
• Greater Superficial Petrosal Nerve (GSPN) → lacrimal glands
• Chorda tympani → submandibular & sublingual salivary glands
3Bad prognostic factors of MS — Types of MS — Predisposing factors of MS
Bad prognostic factors of MS:
• Male sex, onset > 40 years, high lesion load on MRI
• Motor/cerebellar symptoms at onset (vs sensory)
• Frequent relapses, incomplete recovery
• Primary progressive course

Types of MS (courses):
1. Relapsing-Remitting (RRMS) – 85% – most common
2. Secondary Progressive (SPMS) – RRMS that becomes progressive
3. Primary Progressive (PPMS) – 10-15% – progressive from onset
4. Benign MS – mild, limited disability after 15 years

Predisposing factors:
• HLA-DR2 (DRB1*1501)
• Female > Male (2:1), EBV infection history
• Vitamin D deficiency, smoking, high latitude (less sun)
• Age 20-40 years
4Crossed hemiplegia — What is it? Level?
Crossed (Alternating) Hemiplegia = Brainstem lesion

Pattern:
• LMNL of cranial nerve on SAME side as lesion
• UMNL of body on OPPOSITE side

Levels:
• Midbrain: CN3 palsy + contralateral hemiplegia (Weber's syndrome)
• Pons: CN6/7 palsy + contralateral hemiplegia (Millard-Gubler)
• Medulla: CN12 palsy + contralateral hemiplegia
• Lateral medullary syndrome (Wallenberg): CN9,10,11 + ipsilateral face pain/temp loss + contralateral body pain/temp loss + ataxia + Horner's

23 criteria of Parkinson — Red flags of headache — Rebound test
3 Cardinal Features of Parkinson's:
1. Resting tremor (4-6 Hz, pill-rolling)
2. Rigidity (lead pipe/cog-wheel)
3. Bradykinesia (slowness of movement)
+ Postural instability (4th feature)

Red Flags of Headache (SNOOP4):
• Systemic symptoms (fever, weight loss)
• Neurological signs
• Onset sudden (thunderclap = SAH until proven otherwise)
• Onset >50 years
• Positional change (worse lying down = ↑ICP)
• Progressive worsening
• Papilledema
• Precipitated by valsalva

Rebound test: Flex elbow against resistance → release → normal: stops | Cerebellar: hits chest (failure of check reflex)
3Good prognostic factors of MS — Lamotrigine: what is it, use, side effects — Beevor's sign
Good prognostic factors of MS:
• Female, young onset, sensory symptoms at onset
• RRMS course, complete recovery from attacks
• Few lesions on MRI, optic neuritis at onset

Lamotrigine:
• Mechanism: Sodium channel blocker → reduces glutamate release
• Uses: Epilepsy (focal & generalized), Bipolar disorder
• Side effects: Steven-Johnson syndrome (serious!), dizziness, diplopia, rash
• In pregnancy: Safest AED option (though requires monitoring) – preferred over valproate due to teratogenicity

Beevor's sign: Patient lies flat, lifts head → navel moves UPWARD = lower rectus abdominis weakness (T9-10 lesion at T10 level)

1Deep reflexes of lower limbs — Centers — Causes of absent knee reflex — Reflex of chorea — Why?
LL Deep Reflexes:
• Knee: L3, L4 (femoral nerve)
• Ankle: S1, S2 (sciatic → tibial nerve)
• Plantar: S1, S2

Causes of absent knee reflex:
• Peripheral neuropathy (most common – diabetes, GBS, B12 def)
• L3/L4 radiculopathy
• LMN lesion (AHC disease, PN)
• Tabes dorsalis
• Hypothyroidism (slow relaxation), elderly

Reflex in Chorea = PENDULAR REFLEX
Why? Loss of muscle tone → after knee jerk, leg swings like a pendulum multiple times. Reason: hypotonia from loss of striatal control of γ-motor neurones

Hand muscles:

3Optic nerve examination — Light & accommodation reflexes — Color vision test — Differentiating Parkinson's disease vs Parkinson's from arteriosclerosis
Optic nerve exam:
• Visual acuity (Snellen chart)
• Visual fields (confrontation)
• Fundoscopy (optic disc – pallor? papilledema?)
• Pupil: direct & consensual light reflex, accommodation (PERRLA)
• Color vision: Ishihara plates

Light reflex arc:
Afferent: CN2 → pretectal nucleus → bilateral Edinger-Westphal nucleus
Efferent: CN3 (parasympathetic) → ciliary ganglion → constrictor pupillae

PD vs Vascular Parkinsonism:
• PD: Insidious, resting tremor prominent, responds to L-dopa, asymmetric onset
• Vascular: "Lower body" parkinsonism, gait difficulty > tremor, stepwise progression, doesn't respond well to L-dopa, MRI shows ischemic changes
4Complications of subarachnoid hemorrhage — Complication is infarction due to vasospasm
Complications of SAH:
• Rebleeding (peak 24-72h) – most fatal
• Vasospasm → cerebral infarction (most common serious complication, days 4-14)
• Hydrocephalus (obstructive or communicating)
• Hyponatremia (SIADH or cerebral salt wasting)
• Seizures
• Cardiac arrhythmias (neurogenic)

Treatment of vasospasm: Nimodipine (calcium channel blocker), Triple H therapy (Hypertension, Hemodilution, Hypervolemia)

1Reflexes of upper limb — Tone of lower limb — Examine CN 9,10,11,12 — Complications of SAH — Brown-Séquard — Inverted supinator reflex — Red flags of headache
Inverted Supinator Reflex:
• Occurs when C5 lesion is at that level (level lesion)
• Tap brachioradialis → FINGER FLEXION instead of forearm supination
• Mechanism: C5,6 arc disrupted (absent supination) + UMNL below (hyperactive C8 = finger flexors)
• Localizes lesion to C5/C6

Hakim-Adams Syndrome (Normal Pressure Hydrocephalus):
Classic TRIAD: Wet, Wobbly, Wacky
• Urinary incontinence
• Gait ataxia (magnetic gait)
• Dementia
Reversible if treated (VP shunt) – LP test (CSF drainage improves gait)

حالة Parkinson's أسئلة متكررة جداً
1Examine deep reflexes + plantar reflex — What's the findings?
In Parkinson's disease:
• Deep reflexes: Normal (PD is extrapyramidal, not pyramidal → no hyperreflexia)
• Plantar reflex: Flexor (normal) – no Babinski sign
• ⚠️ Important: Positive Babinski → suggests parkinsonism NOT idiopathic PD (vascular, MSA, etc.)

Planter reflex reporting:
Normal = "plantar flexion" (NOT "negative Babinski")
Abnormal = "extensor plantar / positive Babinski sign"
Equivocal = "no clear response"
2Side effects of Dopamine agonists — Treatment of dystonia
Dopamine agonists (Pramipexole, Ropinirole, Cabergoline):
Side effects:
• Nausea, vomiting (most common)
• Orthostatic hypotension
• Hallucinations, psychosis (especially in elderly)
• Impulse control disorders (gambling, hypersexuality)
• Daytime somnolence, sleep attacks
• Peripheral edema

Treatment of Dystonia:
1st line: Botulinum toxin injection (focal dystonia)
Oral: Anticholinergics (trihexyphenidyl), Baclofen, Clonazepam, Tetrabenazine
Surgical: Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS) for generalized dystonia
3Types of Parkinsonism + examples
Primary (Idiopathic): Parkinson's disease (most common ~75%)

Secondary Parkinsonism:
• Drug-induced: Dopamine blockers (metoclopramide, haloperidol, chlorpromazine) – most common secondary cause
• Vascular (lacunar infarcts in basal ganglia)
• Post-encephalitic (encephalitis lethargica)
• Toxic (MPTP, manganese, CO)
• Structural (tumor, hydrocephalus)

Parkinson Plus Syndromes (atypical):
• MSA (Multiple System Atrophy) – autonomic failure + parkinsonism
• PSP (Progressive Supranuclear Palsy) – vertical gaze palsy + falls
• CBD (Corticobasal Degeneration) – asymmetric, alien limb
• DLB (Dementia with Lewy Bodies) – dementia + parkinsonism + hallucinations
4Treatment of Parkinson's + Pathophysiology + How drugs work
Pathophysiology:
Degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in Substantia Nigra (pars compacta) → ↓Dopamine in nigrostriatal pathway → ↑ACh relative → bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor

Treatment:
1. Levodopa/Carbidopa (Sinemet) – GOLD STANDARD
Carbidopa: peripheral dopa-decarboxylase inhibitor → ↑L-dopa reaching brain, ↓peripheral SE
SE: Wearing off, on-off fluctuations, dyskinesias (after years), nausea, postural hypotension

2. Dopamine agonists (Pramipexole, Ropinirole) – used alone early or adjunct

3. MAO-B inhibitors (Selegiline, Rasagiline) – slow progression, mild symptomatic

4. COMT inhibitors (Entacapone, Tolcapone) – extend L-dopa effect, reduce "off" time

5. Anticholinergics (Trihexyphenidyl) – mainly for tremor in young patients

6. Amantadine – mild dopaminergic + anticholinergic, helps dyskinesias

7. Surgical: DBS (subthalamic nucleus or globus pallidus)
5Myerson's sign — What is it? In which CN?
Myerson's sign (Glabellar reflex):
• Tap glabella (between eyebrows) repeatedly
• Normal: blink only on first tap then HABITUATES (stops)
• Abnormal (Myerson's positive): continuous blinking with each tap = NO habituation
• Indicates: Parkinson's disease, frontal lobe lesion
• CN7 (facial nerve) – efferent (blink)
• Afferent: CN5 (trigeminal)
6Location of substantia nigra — Pathogenesis of Parkinsonism
Substantia Nigra location: MIDBRAIN (mesencephalon)
Specifically: dorsal part of midbrain, posterior to cerebral peduncles

Also important: Globus Pallidus internus (GPi) & Subthalamic nucleus (STN)

Pathogenesis:
1. Accumulation of α-synuclein (Lewy bodies) in dopaminergic neurons
2. Oxidative stress → mitochondrial dysfunction → neuronal death
3. Loss of ≥60-70% of SNc neurons before symptoms appear
4. ↓Dopamine → disinhibition of STN → ↑GPi activity → inhibits thalamus → ↓cortical activation = bradykinesia

Subthalamic stroke: Contralateral hemiballismus (violent flinging movements)
7Types of hyperkinetic disorders — Types of myoclonus — Treatment of myoclonus
Types of Hyperkinetic Movement Disorders:
• Tremor – rhythmic oscillation (rest/action/intention)
• Chorea – random, jerky, flowing (Huntington's, drug-induced)
• Athetosis – slow, writhing (CP)
• Ballismus – violent flinging (STN lesion)
• Dystonia – sustained contractions → abnormal postures
• Myoclonus – sudden, brief, shock-like jerks
• Tics – repetitive, suppressible movements/sounds

Types of Myoclonus:
• Physiological (hiccups, hypnic jerks)
• Essential myoclonus (benign)
• Epileptic (JME, progressive myoclonic epilepsy)
• Symptomatic: Metabolic (renal/liver failure), drug-induced, post-anoxic (Lance-Adams)

Treatment of Myoclonus:
• Clonazepam (1st line)
• Levetiracetam (very effective)
• Valproate
• Piracetam (for cortical myoclonus)
8Examination of ocular CN (2,3,4,6) — Most common cranial nerve affected in Parkinson's — What is olfactory affection?
Ocular CN examination:
• CN2: Visual acuity, fields, fundus, RAPD
• CN3: Ptosis, eye deviation (down & out), pupil dilated (if complete CN3 palsy)
• CN4: Vertical diplopia (worse going down stairs), head tilt
• CN6: Horizontal diplopia, eye can't abduct, convergent squint
Examine: Primary position, 9 positions of gaze, cover test

Most common CN affected in Parkinson's:
• CN1 (Olfactory) – anosmia/hyposmia is an EARLY feature of PD, often precedes motor symptoms by years

Glabellar reflex cranial nerves:
• Afferent: CN5 (ophthalmic branch)
• Efferent: CN7 (facial – orbicularis oculi)
9Investigation of stroke — Each occular muscle & its nerve — Treatment mechanism of Parkinson's drugs
Investigation of Stroke:
• CT brain STAT (to exclude hemorrhage, CT is faster)
• MRI brain (DWI – more sensitive for acute ischemia, takes time)
• ECG, cardiac monitoring, Echo (source of embolism?)
• FBC, coagulation, glucose, lipids, CXR
• Carotid Doppler USS

Ocular muscles & nerves:
• CN3 (oculomotor): SR, IR, MR, IO, levator palpebrae + pupil constriction
• CN4 (trochlear): Superior Oblique (SO4) – intort, depress, abduct
• CN6 (abducens): Lateral Rectus (LR6) – abduction only
Mnemonic: LR6SO4 (rest = CN3)

COMT inhibitors (Entacapone):
Block COMT enzyme → prevent L-dopa breakdown in periphery → more L-dopa available → extends duration of L-dopa effect → reduces "wearing off"
10What are the causes of provoked epilepsy?
Causes of Provoked (Acute Symptomatic) Seizures:
• Metabolic: Hypoglycemia (most important!), hypo/hypernatremia, hypocalcemia, hypomagnesemia, uremia, hepatic failure
• Drugs/Toxins: Alcohol withdrawal (most common in adults), cocaine, tramadol, bupropion, fluoroquinolones
• Infections: Meningitis, encephalitis, brain abscess
• Structural: Acute stroke, head trauma, SAH, tumor
• Sleep deprivation, fever, stress, flashing lights
• Eclampsia (pregnancy)
11Types of rigidity — Examination of muscle tone — Tremors examination
Types of Rigidity:
1. Lead-pipe rigidity – uniform resistance throughout movement (basal ganglia)
2. Cog-wheel rigidity – ratchet-like breaks = lead pipe + tremor
3. Spastic rigidity / Clasp-knife – velocity-dependent, UMNL (technically spasticity not rigidity)

Examination of tone:
• Distract patient (ask to count backwards)
• Flex/extend joints passively: elbow, wrist, knee
• For wrist: shaking method (passive flexion-extension)
• Report: normal / ↑(spastic/rigid) / ↓(flaccid)

Tremor examination:
1. Observe at rest (arms on lap) → Resting tremor (PD)
2. Arms outstretched → Postural tremor (essential, physiological)
3. Finger-nose → Intention tremor (cerebellar)

حالة Myotonia أسئلة
1Examine power — Types of Myotonia — Clinical presentation — Definition of Guillain-Barré + how to diagnose
Types of Myotonia:
1. Myotonia Congenita (Thomsen/Becker) – chloride channel defect, improves with warm-up, "warm-up phenomenon"
2. Myotonic Dystrophy (DM1/DM2) – CTG repeat expansion in DMPK gene
• Cold makes it worse, heat improves
• Differentiates: myotonia congenita (no weakness/systemic) vs myotonic dystrophy (weakness + systemic)

Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS):
Definition: Acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (AIDP) – ascending flaccid paralysis

Diagnosis:
• Clinical: Ascending weakness, areflexia, ± cranial nerve involvement
• CSF: Albuminocytological dissociation (↑protein, normal cells)
• NCS: Demyelinating pattern (↓conduction velocity, prolonged F-waves)
• Anti-ganglioside antibodies (anti-GQ1b in Miller-Fisher variant)

In which disease does incrementation occur?
Myasthenic syndrome (Lambert-Eaton) – incremental response on repetitive nerve stimulation at high frequency (opposite to MG which shows decrement)
2Types of Myotonia & differences — Myasthenia Gravis definition & diagnose by decrement test
Myotonia Congenita vs Myotonic Dystrophy:

Myasthenia Gravis:
Definition: Autoimmune NMJ disorder → anti-AChR antibodies (80%) or anti-MuSK (10%)

Decrement test (Repetitive Nerve Stimulation at 3Hz):
• MG: ≥10% decrement in amplitude = POSITIVE (fatigability due to ↓ACh in vesicles)
• Lambert-Eaton: Increment at high frequency (20-50Hz)

Types/Classification: Osserman classification (Ocular, Mild generalized, Moderate, Severe, Crisis)
3Grades of power — Definition of MS — Why affects CNS not PNS?
Grades of Muscle Power (MRC scale):
0 – No contraction
1 – Visible/palpable contraction, no movement
2 – Movement possible, but not against gravity
3 – Movement against gravity, not against resistance
4 – Movement against resistance (4- weak, 4 moderate, 4+ nearly normal)
5 – Normal strength

Multiple Sclerosis (MS):
Definition: Chronic inflammatory demyelinating disease of the CNS

Why CNS not PNS?
• PNS myelin made by Schwann cells (different antigens – MOG, MBP not primary targets in PNS)
• CNS myelin made by Oligodendrocytes – targeted by autoreactive T-cells in MS
• Blood-Brain barrier breach allows access to CNS myelin antigens
• PNS has own immune privilege mechanisms

Clinical picture of MS: Optic neuritis, INO, sensory symptoms, cerebellar signs, Uhthoff's phenomenon (worsening with heat), Lhermitte's sign
4Investigation of MS — CSF (oligoclonal bands) — MRI findings
Investigations of MS (McDonald Criteria 2017):

MRI brain & spine:
• T2/FLAIR: hyperintense plaques – periventricular (Dawson's fingers), juxtacortical, infratentorial, spinal cord
• T1 gadolinium enhancing: active inflammation
• "Black holes" (T1 hypointense): axonal loss

CSF:
• Oligoclonal bands (IgG) in CSF but NOT in serum = strongly supportive (present in 85-90%)
• ↑IgG index
• Normal cell count (or mild pleocytosis)

Evoked potentials:
• VEP (visual): prolonged P100 latency (optic neuritis subclinical)
• SSEP, BAEP: assess other pathways
5Hypertonia causes — Catatonia & myotonia — MS definition & treatment
Hypertonia causes:
• Spasticity (UMNL): stroke, MS, CP, SCI
• Rigidity (extrapyramidal): PD, parkinsonism
• Myotonia (muscle membrane): myotonic disorders
• Catatonia (psychiatric/organic): schizophrenia, metabolic

Treatment of MS (Disease-Modifying Therapy):
• 1st line (RRMS): IFN-β, Glatiramer acetate, Dimethyl fumarate
• Higher efficacy: Natalizumab, Fingolimod, Alemtuzumab, Ocrelizumab
• Acute relapse: IV methylprednisolone 3-5 days
• PPMS: Ocrelizumab (only approved)
• Symptomatic: Baclofen (spasticity), Amantadine (fatigue), Gabapentin (pain)

⚠️ Natalizumab SE: PML (progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy) – JC virus reactivation
6Blood supply of brain — Pyramidal tract & extra-pyramidal tract — UMNL vs LMNL diff & causes of hypertonia
Blood supply of brain:
• Anterior circulation (ICA): ACA (medial hemisphere) + MCA (lateral hemisphere, most of cortex, internal capsule)
• Posterior circulation (Vertebrobasilar): PICA (lateral medulla, cerebellar tonsil), AICA, SCA, PCA (occipital, thalamus)
• Circle of Willis: provides collateral circulation

Pyramidal tract (Corticospinal):
Motor cortex (Betz cells) → internal capsule → cerebral peduncle → decussation at medullary pyramids → lateral corticospinal tract → AHC
Voluntary movement control

Extrapyramidal system:
Basal ganglia → thalamus → motor cortex loop
Controls: initiation, speed, rhythm, coordination of movement
Lesion: Parkinsonism, dystonia, chorea

حالة PN Peripheral Neuropathy
1Full motor examination (UL & LL) — Sensation (type in PN) — Brown-Séquard def & C/P — Dissociated sensory loss
Sensation in Peripheral Neuropathy:
• Pattern: Glove & Stocking distribution (distal > proximal, length-dependent)
• All modalities affected (both small fibers: pain/temp, and large fibers: vibration/proprioception)
• But predominantly depends on which fibers involved (small fiber PN: pain/autonomic; large fiber: ataxia, proprioception loss)

Dissociated Sensory Loss:
Definition: Loss of some modalities with preservation of others
Types:
• Syringomyelia: Lost pain/temp (spinothalamic), preserved touch/proprioception (posterior column) – "cape distribution"
• Brown-Séquard: Ipsilateral loss of proprioception/vibration + Contralateral loss of pain/temp
• Posterior column: Lost vibration/proprioception, preserved pain/temp (tabes dorsalis, B12 deficiency)
• Thalamic: All contralateral modalities lost

Brown-Séquard syndrome: C/P described in dofaa 12 tab above
2Guillain-Barré: manifestation, management — Hemiplegia — Sensory & motor of PN
GBS Clinical Features:
• Preceding infection (Campylobacter jejuni, CMV, EBV, Flu) 2-4 weeks earlier
• Ascending symmetrical flaccid weakness
• Areflexia (universal feature)
• Mild sensory symptoms
• Autonomic dysfunction (arrhythmias, BP fluctuations) – dangerous!
• Respiratory failure – most dangerous complication (monitor FVC)
• Cranial nerve involvement (bilateral facial palsy most common CN)
• Pain (common, often backache)

Management of GBS:
1. ICU if FVC < 20 mL/kg or rapidly declining
2. IVIg (2g/kg over 5 days) OR Plasmapheresis – equal efficacy
3. NO steroids (worsen outcome in GBS)
4. Supportive: Physiotherapy, DVT prophylaxis, pain management, monitor autonomic

GBS vs CIDP: GBS acute (<4 weeks), CIDP chronic (>8 weeks)

• Test light touch/pin-prick from below upward on trunk
• The level where sensation changes from abnormal to normal = sensory level
• Use dermatome map: T4 = nipples, T10 = umbilicus, T12/L1 = inguinal ligament

In cord lesions:
• UMNL below level
• LMN signs at level (due to AHC/nerve root compression)
• Sensory level on trunk
• Bladder/bowel involvement (often)

Why use non-irritant substance for CN1:
Irritant substances (ammonia, peppermint) stimulate CN5 (trigeminal – free nerve endings in nasal mucosa), not CN1
→ Use: coffee, vanilla, cloves, soap

CN1 pathway: Olfactory epithelium → olfactory nerve → cribriform plate → olfactory bulb → olfactory tract → primary olfactory cortex (uncus, piriform cortex)
NO THALAMIC RELAY (unique among sensory CNs)

Optic nerve abnormalities:
• Color vision abnormality (dyschromatopsia) → optic neuritis (MS)
• RAPD (relative afferent pupillary defect) → afferent limb defect
• Pallor of disc → optic atrophy
• Papilledema → ↑ICP (bilateral, no visual loss initially)

Light & Accommodation reflex pathway:
Light reflex: CN2 → Pretectal nucleus → bilateral EW nucleus → CN3 (ciliary ganglion) → sphincter pupillae
Accommodation: Visual cortex → EW nucleus → CN3 → ciliary muscle (constriction, convergence, accommodation)
53rd nerve CN palsy — Nuclei in brainstem — Position of 8th nerve nuclei (Pons) — Complete vs partial ptosis — Differentiate GBS hereditary vs acquired
3rd nerve (CN3) palsy:
Complete: Ptosis + eye "down and out" + dilated fixed pupil
Pupil-sparing CN3 palsy = Medical (ischemic, DM, HTN)
Pupil-involved CN3 palsy = Surgical (posterior communicating artery aneurysm – EMERGENCY)

Degree of ptosis:
• Complete ptosis: upper lid covers pupil entirely (CN3 or sympathetic)
• Partial ptosis: MRD1 (margin-reflex distance) reduced but not zero
• Pseudo-ptosis: dermatochalasis, brow ptosis

CN8 nuclei in Pons:
• Cochlear nuclei (dorsal & ventral cochlear nuclei) – in caudal pons/medulla junction
• Vestibular nuclei (4 nuclei: medial, lateral, superior, inferior) – in pons and upper medulla

GBS: Hereditary vs Acquired:
• Hereditary (CMT – Charcot-Marie-Tooth): insidious onset, pes cavus, family history, NCS shows chronic demyelination
• GBS: Acute, preceding infection, areflexia, CSF albuminocytological dissociation
• GBS CNs: Face most important (bulbar CN – facial bilateral most common)

CN affection in Parkinson's:
• CN1 (olfactory) – anosmia (early pre-motor feature, Braak stage 1-2)

2LL tone + triceps reflex + centre — TTT of Parkinson — MAO inhibitor side effects — Important cranial nerve to examine — Glabellar reflex — Festination
Triceps reflex: C7, C8 (radial nerve)

Festination:
• Gait in Parkinson's: shuffling, small steps, stooped posture
• Festination = accelerating forward with short rapid steps → patient can't stop
• Due to: forward shift of center of gravity + impaired postural reflexes
• Leads to frequent falls (postural instability)

Why olfactory CN important in Parkinson's (most important to examine):
• Anosmia is earliest feature → diagnostic clue
• Differentiates PD from other parkinsonisms
• Also: olfactory testing used in research for early diagnosis

Glabellar reflex in Parkinson's:
• Positive (no habituation) in PD
• Due to frontal lobe dysfunction / subcortical degeneration
• Tested as part of frontal release signs

MAO inhibitor interactions:
• Selegiline + tyramine foods → hypertensive crisis
• Selegiline + meperidine → serotonin syndrome
• Serotonin syndrome with SSRIs/TCAs
3Examination of tone — Brachioradialis reflex and its centers — Radial styloid process landmark — Inverted supinator sign in C5
Brachioradialis reflex:
• Center: C5, C6 (radial nerve)
• Landmark: Radial styloid process (distal radius, lateral wrist)
• Technique: Strike over radial styloid → forearm supination + elbow flexion

Inverted Supinator Reflex:
• Occurs at C5 level lesion
• Strike brachioradialis → FINGER FLEXION (not forearm supination)
• Loss of C5,6 arc (no supination) + UMNL of C8 level (hyperactive finger flexors)
• Key localizing sign for C5/C6 cord/root lesion

What completes at C5 lesion:
• Inverted supinator reflex
• Loss of biceps reflex
• Deltoid weakness (abduction of shoulder)
حالة MND Motor Neuron Disease
4Examine tongue — Examine state of UL — Tone of LL — Tongue fasciculations — MND diagnosis — AHC lesion location — Treatment (symptomatic)
Motor Neuron Disease (MND) / ALS:
Mixed UMNL + LMNL in same patient = hallmark of ALS

Examination findings:
• UL: Wasting + fasciculations (LMNL) + hyperreflexia + Babinski (UMNL)
• Tongue: Wasting + fasciculations (LMNL of CN12) + spastic tongue (UMNL)
• Speech: Dysarthria (bulbar/pseudobulbar)

AHC (Anterior Horn Cell) lesion:
Causes pure LMNL: wasting, fasciculations, flaccid paralysis, areflexia
Diseases: ALS (MND), Polio, SMA, syringomyelia

Diagnosis:
• Clinical (El Escorial criteria)
• EMG: denervation + fasciculations (diagnostic of AHC)
• MRI spine: exclude structural causes
• No specific blood test

Treatment:
• Riluzole – only disease-modifying (glutamate antagonist, extends life ~3 months)
• Edaravone (IV, free radical scavenger)
• Symptomatic: PEG tube (dysphagia), NIV (respiratory failure), Baclofen (spasticity)
• TTT: symptomatic only → imaging to exclude (CT to exclude structural, MRI gold standard)
5Tone UL — Wasting + fasciculations (DD) — Motor aphasia — ALS definition — DD of MND
MND DD (differential diagnosis):
• Multifocal Motor Neuropathy (no UMNL signs, responds to IVIg)
• Cervical myelopathy (pure UMNL, no bulbar, imaging shows compression)
• Kennedy's disease (SBMA – X-linked bulbospinal muscular atrophy)
• Myasthenia Gravis (fatiguable, NMJ, no fasciculations)
• Inclusion Body Myositis

Motor Aphasia (Broca's):
• Non-fluent, effortful speech
• Comprehension relatively preserved
• Repetition impaired
• Lesion: left inferior frontal gyrus (Broca's area, 44/45)

In MND with motor aphasia:
Suggests pseudobulbar palsy (bilateral UMNL of CN9-12) → dysarthria/aphasia-like picture
حالة Facial Palsy LMN
6What are you seeing? LMN facial — Examination — Sensation — Stapedius — Why hyperacusis — Treatment of facial palsy + duration + complications
LMN Facial Palsy (Bell's Palsy) features:
• Loss of wrinkles on affected side
• Patient can't close eye (lagophthalmos) → risk of corneal damage
• Loss of nasolabial fold
• Angle of mouth droops
• Taste loss (anterior 2/3 tongue – chorda tympani)
• Hyperacusis

Hyperacusis mechanism:
• Nerve to stapedius (branch of facial nerve above chorda tympani)
• Stapedius normally dampens tympanic membrane vibration
• When paralyzed → sounds appear excessively loud (hyperacusis)

Treatment of Bell's Palsy:
• Prednisolone 25mg twice daily × 10 days (within 72 hours of onset – most effective)
• ± Acyclovir (if HSV suspected/associated)
• Eye protection: artificial tears, eye patch at night
• Duration: 70% recover fully within 6 months

Complications:
• Synkinesis (abnormal co-movement)
• Hemifacial spasm
• Crocodile tears (gustatory lacrimation – aberrant regeneration)
• Corneal ulceration (if no eye care)

7CN in face — Type of speech — Examination — Babinski sign — First 3 hours treatment — Lesion location — TTT
Types of speech disorders:
• Dysarthria: Motor speech difficulty (slurred/slow/nasal) – no language deficit
• Dysphasia/Aphasia: Language disorder
• Dysphonia: Voice quality disorder (CN10 or cord)

Acute Stroke Treatment (first 3 hours):
1. IV tPA (Alteplase) within 4.5 hours of onset (if ischemic + no contraindications)
2. Mechanical thrombectomy (large vessel occlusion) – up to 24 hours in selected patients
3. Antiplatelet (aspirin 300mg) if thrombolysis not given

Contraindications to tPA:
• Hemorrhagic stroke (CT shows blood)
• Recent surgery/trauma, anticoagulant use
• BP > 185/110 (must treat first)

Conservative treatment:
• Aspirin + Clopidogrel (dual antiplatelet 21 days in minor stroke)
• Statin, antihypertensive, diabetic control
• Physiotherapy, speech therapy, OT
