Why Cherry Red Spot? — the mechanism
CRA occlusion
→
Inner retina becomes oedematous & white
→
Fovea has NO inner retinal layers
→
Fovea stays red (choroid shows through)
The fovea is avascular and thin — the underlying red choroid is visible through it. The surrounding white oedema makes it look like a cherry red spot. This sign disappears after 4-6 weeks as the retina atrophies.
  • Late signs of CRAO: Optic atrophy + vessel narrowing/sheathing + RPE changes
  • Cattle-trucking / box-carring: Segmentation of blood column in arterioles — a dynamic sign seen early
  • 2% of CRAO eyes: Develop Disc / Retinal Neovascularisation
FA findings — sensitivity vs specificity (exam favourite)
Highest Sensitivity
Delay in A-V transit time
Most common FA finding — seen even with partial occlusion
Highest Specificity
Delay in retinal arterial filling
Confirms the arterial side is specifically blocked
  • OCT: Highly reflective emboli plaque within superficial nerve head
  • ERG: Diminished "b" wave (inner retina ischaemia) with normal "a" wave (photoreceptors intact — outer retina OK)
  • Selective "b" wave reduction = classic ERG of CRA occlusion — distinguishes from RD where both waves reduced
Cilioretinal artery — why it matters in CRAO
Present in
~30% of eyes
Origin
Posterior ciliary circulation (NOT the CRA)
FA timing
Fills 1-2 sec BEFORE retinal arteries
When CRAO occurs AND a cilioretinal artery is patent → central vision is preserved. The macular region stays perfused by the ciliary circulation. This gives MUCH better visual prognosis. Isolated cilioretinal artery occlusion can co-occur with CRVO.
CRAO treatment — emergency steps to memorise
Effective only within 24 hours of onset. Goal = dislodge embolus distally + re-establish retinal perfusion before infarction is irreversible.
Ocular
  • Supine posture
  • Ocular massage
  • AC paracentesis
  • Topical: apraclonidine + timolol
  • IV Acetazolamide 500mg
Vasodilation
  • Sublingual isosorbide dinitrate
  • Rebreathing / Carbogen (CO2 vasodilates)
  • Hyperosmotic agents (Mannitol, Glycerol)
  • Nd:YAG laser embolysis (selected)
Systemic
  • Stop smoking
  • Anti-platelet therapy
  • Warfarin if AF
  • Carotid endarterectomy if >70% stenosis
  • Rule out GCA (ESR, CRP)

What CVOS study says
Give PRP AFTER NVI/ANV develops — NOT prophylactically. Anti-VEGF then PRP 1500-3000 burns.
Why NOT prophylactic PRP?
CVOS showed prophylactic PRP did not reduce NVI incidence — only treat when neovascularisation appears.
Key exam point: Follow ischaemic CRVO monthly for anterior segment NVI — at 3 months initially. NVG = poor prognosis, requires urgent anti-VEGF + PRP.
BRVO — AV crossing anatomy + pathogenesis
  • Artery and vein share a common adventitial sheath at the crossing
  • In 99% of BRVO, the artery lies ANTERIOR (in front of) the affected vein
  • Arteriosclerotic artery compresses the vein → turbulent flow → endothelial damage → thrombus
  • >50% of BRVO occur in the superotemporal quadrant

NVI + NVG are MUCH less common in BRVO than CRVO (2-3% at 3 years vs 100-day in ischaemic CRVO). Retinal NVE occurs in ~8%.

Hemi-RVO and Combined CRA+CRV occlusion
Hemi-RVO
  • Variant of CRVO (not BRVO)
  • Affects superior or inferior hemisphere
  • Can be ischaemic or non-ischaemic
  • NVD more common than BRVO
  • Ischaemic HRVO → manage as ischaemic CRVO
Combined CRA+CRV Occlusion
  • Causes similar to CRAO
  • Retrobulbar injection = 1/4 of cases
  • Features of BOTH entities
  • 80% develop Rubeosis + NVG
  • Generally POOR visual prognosis

ROP pathophysiology — two-phase model
Phase 1 — Vaso-obliteration
Premature birth → high O2 exposure → retinal vessels constrict and obliterate → avascular retina develops

Avascular retina → ischaemia → VEGF overproduction → abnormal neovascularisation INTO vitreous → fibrovascular proliferation → RD

Zone I disease — any stage + Plus = Type 1 ROP requiring immediate treatment. More aggressive, more likely to progress to Stage 4-5.

Regression can occur at any stage (especially Stage 1-2). Cicatricial changes after regression: macular ectopia, straightening of vessels, dragged disc, vitreous membranes.
Plus Disease — definition, importance, and Type 1 vs Type 2 ROP
Plus Disease = active, aggressive disease
  • Posterior pole arteriolar tortuosity
  • Venous dilatation
  • Must be present in ≥2 quadrants
  • "Pre-Plus" = borderline changes (not quite Plus)
  • Indicates high VEGF and rapid progression risk
Type 1 ROP → TREAT
  • Zone I — any stage + Plus
  • Zone I — Stage 3 (with or without Plus)
  • Zone II — Stage 2 or 3 + Plus
Type 2 ROP → Watch closely
  • Zone I — Stage 1 or 2, no Plus
  • Zone II — Stage 3, no Plus
